What should a cracked-tooth appointment cover?

A cracked-tooth visit should start with your symptoms and a careful examination, not a promise of one particular treatment before the dentist has looked at the tooth. The dentist may ask when the pain started, whether it hurts when you bite or release your bite, and whether the tooth has been hit or treated before.

The exam may include checking the tooth and nearby gums, testing how it responds to pressure or temperature, and taking dental X-rays. A crack does not always show clearly on an X-ray, so the dentist may use other examination methods as well. Ask what the findings mean and whether more than one option is reasonable.

What might the dentist check before recommending treatment?

The location and depth of a crack matter. A small chip or shallow crack may need a different approach from a crack that reaches the inner part of the tooth or extends below the gumline. The dentist will also consider whether the tooth is loose, whether the nerve is inflamed or infected, and how much healthy tooth remains.

Tell the dentist about swelling, bad taste, fever, recent dental work, and any health conditions or medicines that could affect care. If the diagnosis is uncertain or the crack looks complex, ask whether a referral or another opinion would help before proceeding.

Which treatment options may be offered?

Treatment depends on what the exam shows. A minor chip may be smoothed or repaired with filling material, while a larger crack may need a crown to protect the remaining tooth. If the crack has affected the nerve, root canal treatment followed by a crown may be considered.

Some cracks cannot be repaired well enough to keep the tooth, and extraction may be discussed. Ask the dentist to explain the expected benefit and limits of each option, what could happen if you wait, and whether follow-up care is needed. A cracked tooth generally does not heal itself.

What changes the cost and timing?

The cost depends on the findings and the care recommended. A simple repair is different from a crown, root canal treatment, or extraction and replacement. The number of visits, imaging or other tests, the tooth's condition, and whether a specialist is involved can also affect the total.

Timing depends on pain, swelling, the risk of further damage, and the type of treatment needed. Some care can begin at the examination, while a crown or other restorative work may require additional steps or visits. Ask for a written estimate, what it includes, and whether your insurance plan needs an estimate or authorization first.

What should you avoid before an appointment?

Try not to chew on the affected side, especially if biting causes pain, and avoid hard or sticky foods. Keep the area clean with gentle brushing. If you need to manage discomfort before you are seen, ask a dentist or pharmacist what is appropriate for you, particularly if you have medical conditions or take other medicines.

  • Don't try to glue the tooth or put aspirin directly on it.
  • Don't ignore pain that returns or gets worse, even if it comes and goes.
  • Don't assume a visible line is harmless or that an X-ray alone can rule out a crack.
  • Don't delay asking for care if the tooth broke after an injury or you develop swelling.

When should you seek urgent care?

Call a dentist promptly for a cracked tooth with significant pain, pain when biting, or a broken piece, and tell the office if symptoms are worsening. Seek emergency medical care for trouble breathing or swallowing, rapidly spreading swelling in the face or neck, or a serious injury. Fever with facial swelling also needs urgent assessment.

A dental office can advise whether your symptoms need a same-day appointment or another level of care. If you are unsure, describe the pain, swelling, and any injury when you call rather than waiting for the tooth to settle on its own.
